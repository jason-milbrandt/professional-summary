## Abstract

Fully-coupled air-quality models running in "feedback" and "no-feedback" configurations were compared against each other and observation network data as part of AQMEII-2. In "no-feedback" mode, aerosol direct and indirect effects were disabled. In "feedback" mode, model-generated aerosols were allowed to modify radiative transfer and/or cloud formation processes. Annual simulations were conducted for North America (2006 and 2010) and Europe (2010).

Model-to-model variation in performance was greater than within-model variation associated with feedbacks. However, during summer and intense emission events (e.g., Russian forest fires of 2010), feedbacks had a significant impact on chemical predictions. The aerosol indirect effect usually dominated feedbacks over the direct effect. Direct and indirect effects were often in competition for predictions of ozone, PM, and other species. Feedbacks caused local and regional shifts of ozone-forming chemical regime (NOₓ- vs. VOC-limited). Feedbacks substantially influenced biogenic hydrocarbon emissions and concentrations. The atmospheric transport and chemistry of large emitting sources (forest fires, large cities) were strongly impacted by the presence or absence of feedback mechanisms. Summertime performance for ozone was improved by indirect effect feedbacks; PM performance was degraded, suggesting current parameterizations for in- and below-cloud processes require improvement.

## 1. Introduction

This is the companion paper to Makar et al. (2015a, Part 1). Part 1 examined effects of feedbacks on the models' meteorological predictions; this paper examines effects on chemical predictions. The AQMEII-2 framework and models are described in Part 1.

Key questions addressed:
1. Does incorporating feedbacks result in systematic changes to predicted chemistry and meteorology?
2. Do the changes vary in time and space?
3. To what extent does incorporating feedbacks improve or worsen model results vs. observations?

---

## 2. Methodology

Same modeling framework as Part 1 (GEM-MACH using MY2 microphysics for the Canadian/NA contribution, plus WRF-CHEM, WRF-CMAQ, and European models). The focus here is on how feedback-induced changes to meteorology propagate into changes in chemical predictions.

Observations used: air-quality monitoring networks (ozone, PM2.5, and other species) across NA and EU for the respective simulation periods.

---

## 3. Key Results

### General findings:
- Model-to-model performance differences exceeded within-model feedback effects, consistent with Part 1
- Feedbacks had the largest chemical impacts in **summer** and during **intense emission events**

### Ozone:
- Feedbacks caused local/regional shifts between NOₓ-limited and VOC-limited ozone-forming regimes
- Summertime ozone performance was **improved** by incorporation of indirect effect feedbacks
- Net ozone changes depended on competition between direct (cooling, reduced photolysis) and indirect (cloud changes, altered transport) effects

### Particulate matter (PM):
- PM performance was **degraded** by indirect effect feedbacks, suggesting current in- and below-cloud parameterizations over-predict or under-predict removal rates once aerosol-cloud interactions are made more interactive
- Direct and indirect effects often competed, changing sign of PM response between model configurations

### Biogenic hydrocarbons:
- Feedbacks had substantial influence on biogenic emissions and concentrations
- NA simulations with both direct and indirect effects: summer average isoprene concentrations decreased by up to 10% (due to reduced temperature and radiation)
- EU direct effect simulations during Russian forest fires (2010): grid average isoprene changes of −5 to +12.5%

### Large-scale plumes:
- Transport and chemistry of forest fire and urban plumes were strongly affected by whether feedbacks were active
- Russian forest fire case of 2010 provided a natural experiment for evaluating feedback responses under extreme aerosol loading

---

## 4. Conclusions

- During summer and intense emission events, aerosol-weather feedbacks have significant impacts on chemical predictions of fully coupled models
- The aerosol indirect effect dominates over the direct effect in most chemical response metrics
- Process-level parameterization comparison among fully coupled models is recommended as the next step
- Current in- and below-cloud chemical removal parameterizations should be revisited in the context of aerosol indirect effect coupling
- Feedbacks improve ozone predictions but degrade PM predictions — trade-offs that need reconciliation in next-generation models
