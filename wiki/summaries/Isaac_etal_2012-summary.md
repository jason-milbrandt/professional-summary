## Overview

This paper provides the overview summary of SNOW-V10 (Science of Nowcasting Olympic Weather for Vancouver 2010), a World Weather Research Programme project associated with the 2010 Winter Olympics. The project augmented the observational network around Whistler and Vancouver with specialized instruments, operated a suite of NWP and nowcast systems in real time, and provided research support to operational forecasters during the Games. The paper sets the scene for a Special Issue of Pure and Applied Geophysics containing detailed SNOW-V10 studies.

## Context and Motivation

Nowcasting high-impact winter weather in complex mountain terrain is a major challenge in operational meteorology, and few systems had been tested in such environments before SNOW-V10. The Vancouver 2010 Olympics (held in one of North America's most complex topographic environments) provided a unique, high-profile opportunity to demonstrate and advance this capability. Unlike the Sydney 2000 and Beijing 2008 Olympic projects which mainly demonstrated existing technology, SNOW-V10 combined research and demonstration, making it an international community effort.

## Key Scientific Contributions

- Established and operated an unprecedented multi-platform observational network in complex mountain terrain (>100 sites, specialized instruments including X-band radars, wind profilers, microwave radiometers, disdrometers)
- Demonstrated real-time integration of research NWP models (including EC GEM 1-km LAM and 2.5-km LAM) with nowcast blending systems during a major international event
- Documented important physical phenomena including: diabatic effects of melting snow, terrain-induced flow reversals, diurnal valley circulation, and precipitation modulations by complex terrain
- Quantified nowcast skill across multiple systems using multi-categorical Heidke Skill Scores, showing that observation-blending systems (INTW, INCA) outperformed raw NWP at short lead times
- Demonstrated that human forecasters provided value-added guidance over raw NWP in the first few hours, particularly for temperature and wind gust

## Methods Summary

The project ran from early preparation through the 2010 Olympics (12 February – 28 February) and Paralympics (12–21 March). Real-time products were distributed through a password-protected SNOW-V10 website. A Research Support Desk (RSD) of 2–3 scientists in Vancouver mediated between the Science Team and venue forecasters. Multi-categorical Heidke Skill Scores (HSS) were used for verification against Sport Forecast Tables produced at 15-min resolution for the first 2 hours and hourly thereafter. Key sites included Whistler Mountain Mid Station, Cypress Bowl, Callaghan Valley, and Vancouver International Airport.

## Key Results

- Canadian LAM 1 km model achieved HSS = 0.275 for relative humidity, 0.104 for visibility, and 0.54 for precipitation type at Whistler Mountain Mid Station
- All models struggled with relative humidity (tendency to under-forecast high RH) and wind direction in complex terrain
- Nowcast blending systems (INTW) beat persistence for relative humidity at most sites
- Human forecasters outperformed NWP for temperature (6/6 sites) and wind gust (5/6 sites) at short lead times; NWP performance caught up at longer lead times
- The rapidly changing mountain weather required 1–15 min measurement resolution to capture key events

## Limitations and Caveats

- Verification dataset was limited in size; no formal significance tests were applied
- Statistical verifications over the full Olympic period represent averages; performance in high-impact events (extreme visibility, heavy snow) may differ
- Model representativeness issues at complex terrain sites complicate verification
- This paper is an overview; detailed analyses are in the SNOW-V10 Special Issue

## Relation to Author's Research Program

Milbrandt was a member of the EC Atmospheric Numerical Prediction Research group in Dorval (the same group as Mailhot, Denis, Bélair, Wilson), which was responsible for the Canadian GEM NWP modeling contribution to SNOW-V10. His specific contribution to the SNOW-V10 project included involvement in the high-resolution (1-km) GEM LAM model, which used the Milbrandt–Yau microphysics scheme ⚠ verify. A companion paper in the same Special Issue (Milbrandt, Theriault, and Mo, 2012) — which is his primary first-author contribution to the SNOW-V10 literature — addresses the 1D modeling of phase transitions associated with melting snow, directly relevant to the precipitation type forecasting challenges documented in this overview paper. The SNOW-V10 project reflects the operational-research interface that is central to Milbrandt's work at ECCC.

## Impact and Citations

**Citation count:** ~64 (Semantic Scholar, retrieved 2026-06-06)

This overview paper has become the standard reference for the SNOW-V10 project as a whole, and is cited whenever the SNOW-V10 dataset or the Vancouver 2010 observational network is used. The project produced a rich observational dataset that continues to support research into mountain weather, nowcasting, and winter precipitation processes. The paper is a key reference for the broader WWRP Olympic weather nowcasting initiative.
