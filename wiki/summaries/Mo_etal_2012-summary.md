## Overview

This paper presents a comprehensive observational and modelling analysis of mid-mountain cloud formation at the Whistler alpine venue during the 45-day period surrounding the Vancouver 2010 Winter Olympics. A total of 93 mid-mountain cloud events were identified using webcam imagery and automatic weather station data, and two detailed case studies reveal the dynamical and thermal origins of these clouds. The study proposes a conceptual framework centered on the interaction of a channeled upslope flow with leeward subsidence from a southerly mountaintop wind, providing operational guidance for forecasting these high-impact visibility events in complex terrain.

## Context and Motivation

Mid-mountain clouds ("Harvey's Cloud") at Whistler Mountain caused numerous alpine ski event cancellations and delays before and during the 2010 Olympics. Despite being a recognized hazard, the mechanisms responsible for these clouds were not well understood, and operational meteorologists lacked reliable tools to predict their formation and persistence. Visibility thresholds for alpine competitions (200–500 m) were demanding, and the sparse climatological record in the Whistler area limited guidance options. The SNOW-V10 World Weather Research Programme project assembled international scientists and high-resolution NWP resources to address this gap.

## Key Scientific Contributions

- Compiled the first systematic census of mid-mountain cloud events at a high-altitude alpine venue: 93 events over 45 days (14–17% daylight occurrence frequency, average lifespan 53 min) during the 2010 Olympics
- Established that mid-mountain cloud formation at Whistler is governed by the balance between a channeled southerly upslope flow and leeward subsidence from a southerly mountaintop wind crossing Khyber Cliff — a mechanistic explanation for "Harvey's Cloud"
- Identified a cloud-topped dry layer (driven by downslope subsidence) as a key diagnostic feature that can trigger cloud-top entrainment instability and periodic cloud breakup
- Demonstrated that EC's 1-km GEM LAM model using Milbrandt-Yau microphysics could resolve the detailed mesoscale flow structures driving mid-mountain cloud formation, while also documenting specific model biases (over-forecast ridge strength behind cold fronts, moisture errors in valley)
- Developed a conceptual framework for mid-mountain cloud formation that is transferable to other coast-mountain regions with similar topographic configuration

## Methods Summary

Observational data from the Olympic Autostation Network (OAN: 8 surface stations at 659–2,120 m, 1-min resolution; webcam images at 10-min intervals), Olympic radiosonde station, Doppler radar (VVO), and a Vaisala 915 MHz wind profiler at Squamish were analyzed over 45 days (Feb 5 – Mar 21, 2010). Mid-mountain cloud was defined as a cloud with base above VOT (805 m) and top below RND (1,856 m), identified from VOH-DH webcam imagery.

NWP guidance came from EC's experimental GEM LAM configurations at 15, 2.5, and 1 km horizontal resolution (LAM15k, LAM2.5k, LAM1k), all using the Milbrandt-Yau double-moment microphysics scheme (Milbrandt and Yau, 2005a, b) for in-cloud microphysical and precipitation calculations. Visibility was diagnosed from model output using Gultepe et al. (2009) and Boudala and Isaac (2009) parameterizations.

## Key Results

- **Occurrence statistics:** 93 events; ~50% had lifespan ≤30 min; 11% lasted >2 h; longest event 4.5 h (Feb 24). About 58% of events produced measurable precipitation at VOT. No mid-mountain cloud observed during northeasterly outflow periods.
- **Case 1 (Feb 16):** Cold-frontal passage → southerly inflow channeled to westerly upslope flow → mid-mountain cloud persisting into afternoon; dry layer at VOA confirmed by near-equal mixing ratios at RND (3.30 g/kg) and VOA (3.27 g/kg) with lower RH at VOA; afternoon temperature inversion near mountaintop confirmed leeward subsidence. LAM1k captured morning cloud but over-predicted afternoon drying.
- **Case 2 (Feb 27):** Southerly LLJ (~17 m/s at 2.5 km from profiler) + precipitation-induced down-valley flow → mid-mountain cloud throughout the afternoon; periodic cloud breakup when dry layer at VOA was interrupted. Doppler radar RHI clearly showed growing upslope flow at ~1 km level strengthening as visibility dropped below 300 m. LAM1k successfully predicted afternoon visibility reduction at VOL.
- **Model performance:** GEM LAM with MY microphysics resolved key mesoscale flow structures (Khyber Cliff leeward subsidence, valley channeling), though moisture/ridge strength errors in Case 1 led to premature clearing in the afternoon.

## Limitations and Caveats

- Only cloud events detectable by webcam were included; cloud events during low-light or obstructed conditions were missed
- The study is limited to one season at one location; formal validation of the conceptual framework in other complex-terrain regions remains to be done
- NWP skill for mid-mountain cloud persistence was limited by difficulties in predicting the exact strength and timing of the post-frontal ridge and the LLJ
- Mid-mountain cloud during precipitation events is particularly challenging because multiple visibility-reducing mechanisms co-occur
- The authors note that building rule-based operational forecast tools for mid-mountain cloud identification from NWP output remains future work

## Relation to Author's Research Program

Milbrandt's connection to this paper is through the MY2 microphysics scheme (Milbrandt and Yau, 2005a, b), which served as the cloud microphysics component in all three nested GEM LAM configurations used for this study. The paper documents one of the early high-profile operational applications of the MY2 scheme in a complex-terrain, high-impact weather forecasting context. Milbrandt participated as a member of the SNOW-V10 / EC NWP team (alongside McTaggart-Cowan and Mailhot, also from the Recherche en prévision numérique group). This paper is relevant to his research program as an example of MY2 being used at 1-km resolution for real-time weather forecasting support, validating that the scheme performs adequately at cloud-resolving scales for winter weather applications in complex terrain.

## Impact and Citations

**Citation count:** ~20 (Semantic Scholar, retrieved 2026-06-06)

This paper has accumulated a modest citation count, primarily within the mountain meteorology and orographic cloud community. It is the main reference for mid-mountain cloud phenomenology at Whistler and for the "Harvey's Cloud" mechanism. The conceptual framework for downslope-capping of upslope orographic clouds has potential relevance to similar topographic settings globally.
