## Overview

This paper describes the UNSTABLE (Understanding Severe Thunderstorms and Alberta Boundary Layers Experiment) 2008 pilot field campaign, which targeted convective initiation (CI) and severe thunderstorm processes in the foothills of the Alberta Rocky Mountains. The experiment deployed an extensive suite of fixed and mobile observation platforms during a 15-day intensive observation period (IOP) in July 2008, producing observations of unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution in this region. High-resolution GEM model runs (at 2.5- and 1-km grid spacing) provided NWP support for field operations and for follow-on research into the predictability of CI.

## Context and Motivation

The Alberta foothills are one of Canada's most active severe weather regions, responsible for more than 40 deaths and $2.5 billion (CAD) in storm damage since 1981. The existing synoptic-scale observational network was inadequate for resolving the mesoscale boundaries and ABL processes (moisture gradients, drylines, surface heterogeneity) known to control when and where CI occurs. No targeted field campaign had previously characterized these processes in the Alberta foothills at the required resolution.

## Key Scientific Contributions

- Described the full design, instrumentation, and operations of the UNSTABLE 2008 pilot campaign, including three overarching science questions (ABL processes, surface processes, NWP skill)
- Provided the most complete observational documentation of a dryline in Canada to date, including moisture gradients of 18 g kg$^{-1}$ km$^{-1}$ over distances less than 700 m
- Characterized for the first time the contrasting ABL evolution over adjacent forested and cropped areas in the Alberta foothills, showing that evapotranspiration from crops is a meaningful local moisture source
- Demonstrated that GEM-LAM-1 (1-km) downscaling without additional data assimilation provides limited improvement in CI forecasting over the 2.5-km configuration; better initial conditions are more important than grid spacing alone
- Identified a spurious convection problem in the 2008 GEM-LAM-1 and resolved it for 2009 runs via an updated Charney–Phillips vertical coordinate

## Methods Summary

The field campaign ran 15 June–15 August 2008, with an IOP from 9–23 July. Eight intensive observation days (IODs) were conducted using predefined mission types (dryline, CI-ABL water vapor, CI-mesoscale boundary, water vapor gradient). Platforms included: mobile instrumented vehicles, multiple simultaneous rawinsonde teams, GPS precipitable water sensors, tethersondes, water vapor radiometers, a Doppler sodar, and an instrumented aircraft. NWP support used two GEM limited-area model (LAM) configurations: a 2.5-km run (single-moment MY2005 microphysics) and a 1-km run (double-moment MY2005 microphysics), both driven from the operational 15-km global GEM and run daily in real time.

## Key Results

- Dryline on 13 July 2008 was resolved with dewpoint gradients of 13°C km$^{-1}$ and embedded gradients up to 42°C km$^{-1}$ — far stronger than any signal visible in the operational network alone
- ABL over cropped areas remained cooler, more moist, and shallower than over adjacent forested areas, with $q_v$ differences of ~2.0–2.5 g kg$^{-1}$ at low levels
- The 1-km GEM-LAM sometimes improved simulated convective mode (e.g., linear vs. isolated storms) but not the overall CI forecast without improved initial conditions
- Spurious convection in the 2008 GEM-LAM-1 was essentially eliminated in 2009 after switching to the Charney–Phillips vertical coordinate

## Limitations and Caveats

- The 2008 pilot was deliberately limited in scope; no mobile Doppler radar was deployed (resource constraints), limiting radar-based boundary observations
- Aircraft wind measurements were not usable during the 2008 IOP due to calibration issues
- No data assimilation was performed for either GEM-LAM configuration, limiting the NWP evaluation
- Only one IOD per predefined mission type was completed; more events would be needed for robust statistics

## Relation to Author's Research Program

Milbrandt's contribution to this paper was through the NWP infrastructure: the 1-km GEM-LAM-1 used the Milbrandt and Yau (2005) double-moment bulk microphysics scheme as its explicit condensation parameterization, and the 2.5-km GEM-LAM-2.5 used the single-moment version of the same scheme. The paper's reference to Milbrandt and Yau (2006a,b) in the introduction also acknowledges prior severe storm modeling work in Alberta that helped motivate the NWP component of UNSTABLE. Milbrandt was listed under the NWP Research Section at Environment Canada (Dorval). This paper is peripheral to Milbrandt's primary microphysics scheme development work but reflects the operational deployment of the MY scheme in real-time convective-scale NWP.
