## Overview

This paper introduces the Canadian Arctic Weather Science (CAWS) project, a multi-year ECCC initiative to research future operational monitoring and forecasting for the Canadian Arctic. The project has established a prototype reference observational site at Iqaluit, Nunavut, equipped with a Ka-band Doppler polarization radar, water vapor lidars, Doppler lidars, ceilometers, precipitation sensors, and other instruments. The paper describes the site instrumentation, the GEM-based NWP systems used for forecast evaluation, preliminary observational results, and the project's community engagement approach with the Inuit.

## Context and Motivation

The Canadian Arctic is both sparsely observed and subject to increasing economic and recreational activity — with transpolar air traffic growing rapidly and search-and-rescue incidents much higher than in southern Canada. Future Arctic monitoring networks will require combinations of space-based observations, sparse in situ measurements, and advanced reference sites. CAWS was initiated to define those requirements, test new technologies, validate satellite products, and develop weather forecasting and warning products appropriate for Arctic communities. It aligns with international initiatives including the Polar Prediction Project (YOPP) and the Year of Polar Prediction.

## Key Scientific Contributions

- Establishes the Iqaluit CAWS reference site — the first comprehensive multi-instrument Arctic meteorological observatory of this type operated by ECCC
- Documents the characteristic Arctic weather at Iqaluit: high frequency of snow (32%), blowing snow (17%), and fog; persistent blizzard events (visibility < 0.8 km); very light precipitation (< −5 dBZ) occurring ~40% of winter time in stratified layers up to 7 km height
- Demonstrates multi-instrument profiling capabilities (Ka-band radar, Doppler lidar, radiosonde, HRDPS NWP) to observe and predict fine-scale wind layering during blizzards, revealing up to seven wind direction shifts in the lowest 5 km
- Develops and tests multiple blizzard and fog forecast guidance products (expert rules, regression, Random Forest MOS) using RDPS and HRDPS output
- Demonstrates NWP (HRDPS) wind direction disagreement with observations at Iqaluit, flagging a specific forecasting challenge for the Arctic

## Methods Summary

The Iqaluit site (63°45′N, 68°33′W) was established in fall 2015 with instruments added over 2015–2018. NWP systems evaluated include the operational RDPS (10 km) and HRDPS (2.5 km) and the experimental CAPS (3 km). Time–height displays from radar (VAD analysis), lidar (DBS), radiosonde, and NWP are compared for wind speed and direction. Blizzard statistics are computed from 23 October 2015 to 2 April 2018. Weather observation frequencies from 2016–18 are presented. Forecast guidance products are described including regression-based, rule-based, and Random Forest classifiers.

## Key Results

- Iqaluit has notably high frequencies of snow (32%), blowing snow (17%), and fog; blizzard events are persistent and can last days
- Very light precipitation layers occur ~40% of winter time, extending up to 7 km — difficult to observe or model with conventional NWP systems
- HRDPS wind speeds are generally consistent with Ka-band radar, lidar, and radiosonde, but wind directions show disagreement
- Multiple blizzard and fog guidance products were developed from NWP output and tested; Random Forest MOS and regression approaches show useful skill
- Observations reveal fine-scale atmospheric layering not captured by NWP: up to 7 wind direction shifts in the lowest atmosphere during blizzard events
- Limited forecast products were made available to Edmonton and Winnipeg weather offices and well received

## Limitations and Caveats

- At the time of publication, Internet bandwidth constraints at Iqaluit limited real-time data transfer; raw data was retrieved manually
- Most instruments were commercial off-the-shelf and not specifically rated for Arctic environments
- The paper is primarily a site description and overview; detailed scientific analyses appear in subsequent CAWS publications
- Community engagement with the Inuit is described as a recognized challenge; co-design products are at an early stage

## Relation to Author's Research Program

Milbrandt is listed as an ECCC co-author on this project overview. His primary connection to CAWS is through the NWP evaluation component: the HRDPS (2.5 km) and CAPS (3 km) models, which incorporate the P3 microphysics scheme he co-developed (Morrison and Milbrandt, 2015; Milbrandt and Morrison, 2016), are the operational models used for forecast validation in the project. The CAWS project provides an Arctic context for evaluating the performance of Canadian NWP systems including their microphysics parameterizations — a direct extension of Milbrandt's research program into the polar forecast domain.
