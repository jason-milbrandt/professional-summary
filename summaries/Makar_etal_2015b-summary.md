## Overview

This is the companion paper to Makar et al. (2015a), examining how aerosol–weather feedbacks in fully coupled air-quality/weather models affect the models' chemical predictions rather than their meteorological predictions. Using the same AQMEII-2 framework, the study found that during summer and intense emission events, feedbacks significantly impact chemical forecasts. The aerosol indirect effect generally dominated, and direct and indirect effects were often in competition for ozone and PM predictions. Feedbacks improved summertime ozone performance but degraded PM performance, pointing to specific parameterization weaknesses in current coupled models.

## Context and Motivation

Part 1 established that aerosol–weather feedbacks alter meteorological forecasts in systematic but model-dependent ways. This paper asks whether those meteorological changes have downstream consequences for predicted chemistry — ozone, PM2.5, biogenic hydrocarbons — and whether including feedbacks improves or degrades air-quality forecast skill. This is the critical practical question for deciding whether to invest in full coupling for operational forecasting systems.

## Key Scientific Contributions

- Demonstrated that aerosol–weather feedbacks produce significant chemical prediction differences during summer and intense emission events, even when annual mean impacts are modest
- Showed that feedbacks cause local/regional shifts in ozone-forming chemical regime (between NOₓ-limited and VOC-limited environments), with implications for air-quality management
- Documented a 10% reduction in summer average isoprene concentration in NA simulations with both feedback types, driven by feedback-induced cooling and radiation reductions
- Showed that Russian forest fire plumes in 2010 provided a striking example of strong feedback sensitivity in large aerosol loading events
- Found that indirect effect feedbacks improved ozone performance but degraded PM performance, pointing to current parameterization gaps in cloud scavenging and below-cloud removal processes

## Methods Summary

Same framework as Part 1. The Canadian contribution used GEM-MACH with MY2 microphysics for the NA domain. Chemical observations from surface monitoring networks (ozone analyzers, PM2.5 monitors, speciated chemistry) across NA and EU were used for verification. Statistical metrics included annual and seasonal bias, standard deviation, and correlation.

## Key Results

- Model-to-model performance differences exceeded within-model feedback effects (consistent with Part 1)
- Summer and intense emission events showed the largest chemical feedback responses
- Direct and indirect effects often competed: sign of chemical response depended on which effect dominated
- Feedbacks caused NOₓ/VOC regime shifts in ozone chemistry, with management implications
- Summertime ozone performance improved with indirect effect feedback inclusion
- PM2.5 performance degraded with indirect effect — over-/under-prediction of cloud/below-cloud scavenging
- Summer average isoprene decreased up to 10% in NA (feedback-induced cooling and radiation reduction)
- Russian forest fire plumes showed strong, large-area feedback responses in isoprene and other biogenic chemistry

## Limitations and Caveats

- Same model resolution limitations as Part 1 (GEM-MACH at 15 km)
- Process-level attribution not conducted; it remains unclear which specific parameterizations drive the PM degradation
- The PM result suggests that in-cloud and below-cloud scavenging parameterizations require dedicated intercomparison and improvement before full coupling can reliably improve PM forecasts
- Russian fire episode is an extreme event; generalizability of fire-plume results is uncertain

## Relation to Author's Research Program

Milbrandt's role in this paper is the same as in Part 1: providing the MY2 microphysics scheme within GEM-MACH for the NA simulations. The indirect effect mechanism required a two-moment cloud scheme (MY2) to represent how aerosol loading modifies droplet number concentration and hence cloud optical properties and scavenging rates. Both Makar et al. (2015a,b) demonstrate that MY2 was being used in the context of fully coupled chemistry-weather modeling — a domain distinct from Milbrandt's core NWP microphysics work, but one where the quality of the microphysics parameterization was directly relevant to the scientific questions being asked.

## Impact and Citations

**Citation count:** ~128 (Semantic Scholar, retrieved 2026-06-06)

Highly cited alongside its companion paper, reflecting the significance of the AQMEII-2 intercomparison for the coupled air-quality/NWP modeling community. Together, the two Makar et al. (2015a,b) papers have been widely used as reference points for evaluating aerosol–weather feedback strength across modeling platforms and for motivating improvements to indirect effect parameterizations in coupled systems.
