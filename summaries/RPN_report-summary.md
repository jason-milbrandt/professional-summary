## Overview

This is an invited review article documenting the six-decade history of Recherche en Prévision Numérique (RPN) — the NWP research group within ECCC's Meteorological Research Division — and its contributions to Canadian and international NWP. The paper covers numerical methods (semi-Lagrangian, semi-implicit schemes), the GEM model's development, data assimilation evolution, ensemble systems, current operational prediction systems (GDPS, RDPS, HRDPS), and future directions. It was published in Atmosphere-Ocean to mark the 150th anniversary of the Meteorological Service of Canada.

## Context and Motivation

RPN has been a world-leading NWP research group since the 1960s, pioneering semi-Lagrangian time integration schemes (André Robert) that are now used globally. As the MSC reached its 150th anniversary in 2021, the Atmosphere-Ocean journal invited this comprehensive review to consolidate the history of RPN's innovations and provide a reference for the broader meteorological community. The paper spans from André Robert's first barotropic model (1963) through the current coupled atmosphere–ice–ocean GEM-based prediction systems.

## Key Scientific Contributions

- Historical overview of Canadian NWP from 1963 to the present, covering filtered models, primitive-equation models, and GEM
- Documents the evolution of data assimilation systems: from objective analysis and OI through 3D-Var and 4D-Var
- Reviews the development of global, regional, and high-resolution deterministic prediction systems (GDPS, RDPS, HRDPS)
- Describes the HRDPS physics upgrade (September 2018) that replaced the MY2 microphysics scheme with the P3 scheme, noting improved precipitation skill scores in mountainous regions
- Reviews ensemble NWP systems developed at RPN (GEPS, REPS)
- Outlines future directions: height-based terrain-following coordinates, earth system modelling, scalable numerical methods

## Methods Summary

Review article — no original research methods. The paper synthesizes six decades of peer-reviewed publications, operational model changes, and technical reports from the RPN group and CMC. It is structured chronologically with topical sections for numerical methods, data assimilation, GEM development, current systems, ensembles, and future outlook.

## Key Results

Not applicable (review article). Key historical milestones documented:
- 1963: First Canadian operational NWP model (barotropic, André Robert)
- 1980s: Semi-Lagrangian semi-implicit schemes — a global paradigm shift in NWP
- 1997: GEM model introduced for operations
- 2014–2017: HRDPS developed and made operational (2.5 km, pan-Canadian)
- September 2018: P3 microphysics scheme replaced MY2 in HRDPS, improving precipitation skill in mountainous regions
- 2017: First medium-range coupled atmosphere–ice–ocean operational forecast system globally (GDPS)

## Limitations and Caveats

- As a review article, it is inherently not comprehensive — the focus is on RPN contributions; other Canadian and international contributions are cited but not reviewed in depth
- The paper reflects the institutional perspective of RPN scientists; selection of highlighted contributions reflects the authors' collective judgment
- Some future systems described (height-based coordinates, earth system modelling) remain research-stage as of the publication date

## Relation to Author's Research Program

Milbrandt is listed as one of 21 co-authors, all from ECCC/RPN. His contribution to this review reflects his standing as a leading RPN scientist specializing in cloud microphysics parameterization. The paper documents that the MY2 scheme (Milbrandt & Yau, 2005) was the operational microphysics scheme in the HRDPS for nearly a decade, and that its replacement with P3 (Morrison & Milbrandt, 2015) in September 2018 improved precipitation skill scores. This is a direct institutional acknowledgement of Milbrandt's contributions to the operational NWP system. The paper also cites the Milbrandt et al. (2016) HRDPS system description paper (one of Milbrandt's Tier 1 papers) as the technical reference for the 2.5 km system. This review article provides useful context for the broader significance of Milbrandt's microphysics research within the Canadian NWP programme.
