## Abstract

A comprehensive study of mid-mountain clouds and their impacts on the Vancouver 2010 Winter Olympics and Paralympics is presented. Mid-mountain clouds were frequently present on the Whistler alpine venue, as identified in an extensive archive of webcam images over a 45-day period from February 5 to March 21, 2010. These clouds posed serious forecast challenges and had significant impacts on some Olympic and Paralympic alpine skiing competitions.

Under fair weather conditions, a diurnal upslope (anabatic) flow can work in concert with a diurnal temperature inversion aloft to produce a localized phenomenon known as "Harvey's Cloud" at Whistler. Two detailed case studies suggest that mid-mountain clouds can also develop as a result of a moist valley flow interacting with a downslope flow descending from the mountaintop. A southerly inflow through the Sea-to-Sky corridor can be channeled by the local topography into a westerly upslope flow toward Whistler Mountain, resulting in orographic clouds on the alpine venue. Under favorable circumstances, these clouds are trapped to the mid-mountain zone by the leeward subsidence of an elevated southerly flow. The presence of the downslope subsidence was manifested by a distinguished dry layer observed on the top of the mid-mountain clouds in both cases. It is the subsidence-induced adiabatic warming that imposes a strong buoyant suppression to trap the mid-mountain cloud. The subsidence-induced dry layer has the potential to trigger evaporative instability to periodically break up the mid-mountain cloud.

## 1. Introduction

Forecasting orographic cloud poses a great challenge in areas of complex terrain where meteorological conditions exhibit dramatic spatial and temporal variability. During the Vancouver 2010 Olympics, mid-mountain clouds ("Harvey's Cloud") frequently impacted alpine competitions at the Whistler alpine venue. Visibility thresholds for alpine competitions were set between 200 and 500 m. The Olympic Forecast Team (OFT) of Environment Canada identified accurately predicting visibility as one of the greatest challenges.

The SNOW-V10 (Science of Nowcasting Olympic Weather for Vancouver 2010) project organized an international science team to work side-by-side with the OFT. A companion NWP effort (Mailhot et al., 2010, 2012) used EC's experimental high-resolution mesoscale prediction system with the **Milbrandt-Yau double-moment microphysics scheme** (Milbrandt and Yau, 2005a, b) in all three nested model configurations.

## 2. Geography, OAN Data, and Model Outputs

### 2.1 The Whistler Alpine Venue

Whistler Mountain (summit ~2,182 m) is located ~125 km north of Vancouver. All alpine skiing events were held at Whistler Creekside, with courses descending from 1,684 m (men's downhill, Dave Murray slope) and 1,595 m (women's events, Franz's Run) to Timing Flats (805 m).

### 2.2 The Olympic Autostation Network (OAN)

Eight automatic weather stations equipped with pressure, temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, and visibility sensors at 1-min intervals, plus high-resolution digital webcams archived every 10 min:
- VOC (Nesters, 659 m), VOT (Timing Flats, 805 m), VOB (Whistler Creekside, 933 m)
- VOL (Whistler Mt. Mid Level, 1,320 m), VOA (Whistler Mt. High Level, 1,640 m)
- VOH (Whistler Mt. High Level Wind, 1,643 m), RND (Roundhouse Helipad, 1,856 m), PEK (Whistler Peak, 2,120 m)

Also: Whistler Olympic radiosonde station (co-located with VOC), Doppler radar (VVO, 557 m), Vaisala 915 MHz wind profiler at Squamish airport (WSK).

### 2.3 EC's High-Resolution NWP Model Outputs

Three nested GEM LAM configurations at 15, 2.5, and 1 km horizontal grid spacing (LAM15k, LAM2.5k, LAM1k), driven by REG15k (15-km regional model). Hybrid terrain-following 58-level vertical coordinate, with ~10 levels in the first kilometer above ground. Run twice daily. All three LAM configurations used the **Milbrandt-Yau double-moment microphysics scheme** (Milbrandt and Yau, 2005a, b) for in-cloud microphysical and precipitation calculations. Diagnostic visibility outputs were derived from NWP outputs using parameterizations from Gultepe et al. (2009) and Boudala and Isaac (2009).

## 3. Mid-Mountain Clouds During the 2010 Olympics

### 3.1 Definition

Mid-mountain cloud: orographic cloud with base touching the mid-mountain slopes. In this study, operationally defined as a cloud attaching to the alpine venue with base above VOT (805 m) and top below RND (1,856 m).

### 3.2 Observations

Total of **93 mid-mountain cloud events** identified during daylight hours over the 45-day study period:
- Roughly half had lifespan ≤30 min; 11% lasted >2 h; longest was 4.5 h (Feb 24, 1100–1530 PST)
- Average lifespan: 53 min
- About 58% of events produced measurable precipitation at VOT
- Overall occurrence frequency: **14%** during the full daylight period; **17%** during 0630–1830 PST
- Weak late-morning tendency (around 1100 PST) related to valley airflow morning transition
- No significant mid-mountain cloud during northeasterly outflow periods (Feb 17–22, Mar 18–20)
- Most events occurred with stably stratified boundary layer and weak-to-moderate southerly inflow through the Sea-to-Sky corridor

## 4. Case Studies

### 4.1 Mid-Mountain Clouds Due to Southerly Inflow: February 16, 2010

Following a cold-frontal passage, a ridge of high pressure offshore established southerly inflow in the Sea-to-Sky corridor. Mid-mountain clouds formed behind the cold front and lingered through the afternoon:

- Visibility at VOL reduced to ~100 m within the cloud
- Soundings showed a dry layer at the VOA level developing after 1030 PST — manifested as a cloud-topped boundary layer
- The dry layer resulted from downslope subsidence (downslope flow from RND to VOA confirmed by near-constant mixing ratio, ~3.28 g/kg at both stations, with decreased RH at VOA purely from adiabatic warming)
- The afternoon temperature inversion near mountaintop (consistent with subsidence theory) made it very difficult for the orographic cloud to penetrate from below
- LAM1k model predicted mid-mountain cloud formation correctly through noon, but failed to predict afternoon persistence — the model over-predicted the ridge strength behind the cold front, leading to too-rapid drying

### 4.2 Mid-Mountain Clouds Due to Southerly Low-Level Jet: February 27, 2010

A deep upper-level trough along the coast created unstable conditions with a southerly LLJ (~17 m/s at 2.5 km level, observed at WSK profiler) and precipitation-induced down-valley flow:

- Mid-mountain clouds observed throughout the afternoon despite heavy precipitation in morning
- A dry layer at the VOA level periodically broke down when clouds grew vertically
- Doppler radar RHI displays showed a persistent red layer (away-from-radar/upslope flow) developing in the morning just above the 1-km level at VOL, growing stronger as visibility at VOL reduced below 300 m
- Northeast winds near valley bottom — down-valley flows induced by diabatic cooling from hydrometeor evaporation within the dry layer
- LAM1k successfully predicted the visibility reduction at VOL after 1000 PST; generally captured the flow structure (northerly down-valley flow in morning, southerly inflow in afternoon), though mountaintop winds weakened earlier than observed

## 5. Conceptual Model

The thermal and dynamical origin of mid-mountain cloud in the Whistler area is captured in a schematic framework (Fig. 22):

**Key ingredients:**
1. **Channeled upslope flow:** Southerly inflow through the Sea-to-Sky corridor is channeled by local topography (VOL area) into westerly toward-Whistler-Mountain flow → orographic uplift → moisture condensation and cloud formation on alpine slopes
2. **Leeward downslope subsidence:** Southerly mountaintop wind across the southwestern ridge of Whistler Mountain (Khyber Cliff) produces a leeward subsidence → subsidence-induced adiabatic warming below the ridge level → stable layer that creates negative buoyancy suppressing the upslope flow → traps the orographic cloud to the mid-mountain zone
3. **Cloud-topped dry layer:** The downslope subsidence creates a dry layer at the cloud top, which can trigger cloud-top entrainment (evaporative) instability to periodically break up the underlying cloud
4. **Mountain wave control:** A very weak mountaintop wind cannot trap orographic clouds; a strong mountaintop wind (Chinook-type) can completely disperse them; an intermediate strength produces the mid-mountain cloud regime
5. **Flow reversal:** A stably stratified valley flow can be partially blocked and split into an upslope component (cloud-forming) and a reversed surface flow (drying the valley bottom)

Key contrast with banner clouds: the upslope flow here is dynamically driven by valley inflow, not by a leeward upwelling dynamically induced by cross-barrier flow over a sharp mountain peak.

## 6. Conclusions

- 93 mid-mountain cloud events identified at Whistler during the 2010 Olympics (Feb 5 – Mar 21); 14–17% daylight occurrence frequency
- Mid-mountain clouds form through the interplay of channeled upslope flow (valley inflow) and leeward subsidence capping (from southerly mountaintop flow)
- A cloud-topped dry layer driven by downslope subsidence can trigger evaporative instability and periodic cloud breakup
- EC's LAM1k mesoscale NWP model (using Milbrandt-Yau microphysics) generated dynamically consistent guidance for operational forecasting over complex terrain, but errors in the strength and timing of the ridge/LLJ could degrade the forecast for mid-mountain cloud persistence
- SNOW-V10 nowcasting tools provided real-time support to the OFT for model validation and short-range decision-making
- The conceptual framework is transferable to other regions with similar coast-mountain topography (e.g., Columbia and Kootenay districts, BC)
